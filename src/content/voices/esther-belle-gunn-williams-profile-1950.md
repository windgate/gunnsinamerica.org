Always in moderation—“I always quit before I am satisfied,” she admitted.

She gets around with ease and daily goes up and down the stairs in her home. When the weather permits, she can be found every Sunday morning in her place at Sunday School and church in the First Baptist Church of La Salle, of which she has been a lifelong member.

To point up the part the church activities have played in her life, she was the organist for many years, married the minister’s son, who was the soloist and superintendent of the Sunday School. But that is getting away ahead of the story. Mrs. Williams’ life goes back to the early days of La Salle history. In fact no history of La Salle nor Bureau county would be complete without the contribution of her father and family.

Mrs. Williams’ father, Aaron Gunn of Montague, Mass., was a member of a colony formed in 1830 and came to what is now La Salle in 1831. Her father, with some young men, bought two canoes at Mottville, Michigan and floated down the St. Joseph river to South Bend, Indiana. Getting the canoes to the Kankakee river, they floated down the Kankakee to the Illinois river and landed at Hennepin. The settlement did not succeed at Hennepin and Mr. Gunn bought a claim in the vicinity of La Moille in Bureau county. In 1835 he sold his claim and bought 400 acres of what is now north La Salle. Gunn avenue in La Salle is named for the family. Interested in church work he did much preaching in the churches of that day. A leader in community betterment and higher education he gave land for a school of higher education in La Salle. His son Moses was the first white child born in La Salle.

Mrs. Williams’ husband, Thomas A. Williams, whom she married in 1879, was the principal dairyman in La Salle many years. He was born in Devonshire, England, in 1849, the son of the Rev. and Mrs. Thomas Williams, and with them came to the United States in 1874. His father was pastor of the La Salle Baptist church. He was the Sunday School superintendent, and singer and there he met his bride, the organist, the heroine of our story.

“History of La Salle County,” published in 1886, says of Mr. Williams, who died in 1902, “His dairy farm consists of 165 acres and he milks on an average of 34 cows, disposing of his milk in La Salle.”

The house still stands on Creve Coeur street, where Mrs. Williams, as Esther Belle Gunn, was born, the youngest of seven children. She attended her first school where Washington school now stands. She received, what, in that day, was considered a high school education and was awarded a scholarship to the Hillsdale Baptist college in Hillsdale, Michigan where she received her higher education. After her marriage she lived in La Salle where her husband had a large dairy farm. They moved to Florida, near Tallahassee, where they owned and operated a plantation of 500 acres. This plantation was near the Florida State College. Mr. Williams died in 1902 and she moved back to La Salle in 1903. Mrs. Williams has one son, Roy, Peoria, and a daughter, Mrs. Homberg, Peru. She has three grandchildren and five great-grandchildren.

She watches with interest the basketball honors which her grandson-in-law, Gene Vance, Peru, has received. He gained national prominence in collegiate basketball circles as a member of the immortal Whiz Kids at the University of Illinois, who not only won the Big Ten conference title, but set records which still stand. After his graduation he joined the Chicago Stags of the National League. He now is a member of the Tri-City Blackhawks at Moline, a member of the newly organized National American Basketball league.

There are many interesting stories which Mrs. Williams might tell having lived through four major wars, a world-wide depression, saw the completion of two half centuries, and the turn of a century.

When asked what she thought of the atom bomb, she expressed no fear of its consequences and quoted her favorite verse, Psalm 34:7, “The angel of the Lord encampeth round about them that fear him, and delivereth them.”

She remembers well the events of the Civil War. In a talk with her father she asked why could not the North let the South have its own government and stop all this killing of peoples. Her father went into great detail to convince her of the North’s cause and one thing which she remembered was, “We would not want a king in New Orleans.”

While the Civil War seemed near home, Mrs. Williams recalled that the Spanish-American War was even nearer as at that time she was living near the midst of its activities on the Florida coast.

Recalling the days when La Salle had no lights she told how kerosene lanterns were carried to church and used in the church. In 1882 she made a visit to Chicago and with a group someone suggested riding down State street. She remarked it would be dark down that street, but she came in for a surprise for it was then she saw her first electric lights. She also recalled coming home on the Rock Island railroad when one of the passengers was very upset because the “train must be going 40 miles an hour.”

“My real hobby,” said Mrs. Williams, “was my flower garden. I had roses from June until late autumn, delphinium, baby’s breath, and more.” She likes to sew and still does the mending.

Music is another hobby, dearly loved and still practiced by Mrs. Williams. She was the church organist at an early age and studied harmony in college. She still plays all her favorite hymns from memory and even plays when she is indisposed, she never feels too ill to play the piano.

Her church has claimed her whole heart from her girlhood even until now. All activities of the church are very precious to her and she is known and loved by all its people. For many years, so long as she was physically able to keep up the work she taught a Sunday school class and took an active part in the Missionary society and class activities.

Recalling early church history, she remembered that the local Baptist church was organized in the home of a Congregationalist, a Mr. Lathrop in Rockwell. In the days when she was a young woman, the Protestant churches all were united to the extent that when one had what was then called a “protracted” meeting the others dispensed with services and “came together” for the services. These services lasted all winter, she recalled, usually starting about Christmas and continuing nightly until March. One year it would be in the Baptist church another in the Methodist and then again in the Congregational. She was baptized in the Vermilion river.

Her favorite radio station is WMBI of the Moody Bible Institute, Chicago, and she never missed a broadcast of the recent Founders’ Week conference. She is one of the few now living, and especially in this community, who heard D. L. Moody, the evangelist and founder of the institute. She heard him preach in Ottawa.

A humorous note which she recalled was the remark of the preacher in her early girlhood when he would say, “I am always reminded when preaching that I have in my audience both Moses and Aaron.” (Moses and Aaron were brothers of Mrs. Williams.)

The Bible is Mrs. Williams’ greatest treasure and most read and loved book. She likes and reads a great deal of history, but the Bible—through annually the past 20 years, since she was 80. “I started to read it by the calendar schedule,” she said, “but I had more time and I thought I might as well read faster and as a result I was always through before the year ended.” The Gospel according to John is her favorite book.

Mrs. Williams pointed out that in her family there are a number who have lived long lives. Her father lived to be 92, her mother to 99, and she had a cousin who lived to be 100 years.

Celebrating a birthday is nothing new to her, as Mrs. Homberg has taken care to have a small birthday celebration for the past 20 years for her mother. When she was 90, the pastor, the Rev. G. S. Hamilton, announced over the radio, the honor of having Mrs. Williams in the church service, and without any former preparation Mrs. Williams recognized the honor accorded her by giving a brief Christian testimony which was heard by the listening audience, although she was not near the microphone.

This writer, as her present Sunday school teacher feels very humble and greatly honored when Sunday after Sunday one so well read in the Bible is in class and anxiously waiting to learn even more from God’s Word.
