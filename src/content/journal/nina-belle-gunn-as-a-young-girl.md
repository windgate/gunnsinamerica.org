I remember this photo hanging on our kitchen wall as I was growing up. I loved looking at it. It reminds me of home. One day I asked my mother who this was. She said it was "her mother when she was young."

I never met Nina Belle Gunn, sadly. She died in 1941, and I didn't appear until 1951. I could not imagine my mother having a mother, so connecting "Mom" to an old photo of a young girl was difficult. My mother died in 1972, and I never asked the important questions I should have. Knowing more about this photo would have been a seriously good question.

Below is a summation of the photo and its restoration.

## Probably c. 1886–1890

This unmarked family portrait is traditionally identified as Nina Belle Gunn (1877–1941). The identification rests on family provenance: the portrait remained in the Pyle/Lumpp household, and Catherine Joan Pyle, Nina Belle's daughter, was remembered as identifying the image as a photograph of her mother. Although that identification has not been independently documented, the apparent age of the subject is consistent with Nina Belle during the late 1880s.

The portrait is mounted on celluloid, an early plastic widely used for photographic novelties and decorative portrait pieces from the late nineteenth into the early twentieth century. Celluloid commonly yellows, becomes brittle, and develops cracking or "crazing" with age, all of which are visible here. The large circular format suggests that it may originally have been produced as a photo medallion, large pinback-style portrait, or stand-up studio piece, of a type marketed by firms such as the Columbia Medallion Studio and similar companies around the turn of the century.

The sepia-toned profile, flowing hair, and decorative presentation are characteristic of portrait styles associated with the late Victorian, Art Nouveau, and early Edwardian periods. The physical object itself may therefore date somewhat later than the original photograph from which the portrait image was made.

## About the restoration

I approached the digital restoration conservation-first. I preserved the surviving facial features, hair, expression, and photographic detail. I limited my intervention to identifiable physical deterioration, including cracks, flaking, staining, glare, and surface damage. Where information was lost, I used adjacent photographic texture conservatively rather than inventing new facial or physical detail.
