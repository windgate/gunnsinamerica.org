# Ensign Jobamah “Job” Gunn

## 1637–1715

**Roxbury, Massachusetts Bay Colony → Milford, New Haven and Connecticut Colonies**

Jobamah “Job” Gunn belonged to the first American-born generation of the Gunn family. Born at Roxbury, Massachusetts Bay Colony, about 1637, he was a son of Dr. Jasper Gunn and Mary Christian Baldwin Gunn. His parents had crossed the Atlantic during the Great Migration; Job belonged to the generation that converted their uncertain colonial beginning into a permanent New England inheritance.

By the early 1660s, Job had joined the Gunn family at Milford, then part of the New Haven Colony. On 30 October 1663, he married **Sarah Lane**, born in 1642, whose family connections further established him within Milford’s network of households. Together, Job and Sarah had six known children: Samuel, Elizabeth, Sarah, Christian, Abel, and Nathaniel.[^1]

Job’s surviving children carried the family securely into the eighteenth century. Samuel later held the ranks of lieutenant and captain, while Abel was known as a sergeant. The name Christian, given to one of their daughters, may have preserved the memory of Job’s mother, Mary Christian Baldwin Gunn.

Although Job’s importance was once understood chiefly through his descendants, Milford’s town records reveal that he also held recognized responsibilities within the community. By the 1690s he was styled **Ensign Jobamah Gunn**, identifying him as a junior commissioned officer in the local militia.[^2] The rank was more than ceremonial: militia organization formed an essential part of local government and community defense in colonial Connecticut.

Job also participated directly in Milford’s civil administration. Town records name him among the men chosen as **listers**, responsible for helping determine taxable property, and as an **auditor of the town accounts**. He was appointed to inspect and lay out parcels of land and to report upon proposed grants, responsibilities that required the confidence of his neighbors and a working knowledge of the town’s property boundaries.[^3]

His standing became still clearer when Milford elected him **town treasurer**. The record names “Ensign Jobamah Gunn” as treasurer for the ensuing year and grants him a salary of forty shillings. He was subsequently chosen again, indicating that his service was not incidental but part of an established public role.[^4]

In 1712–1713, Jobamah was named among the Milford freeholders and proprietors whose rights were confirmed by a patent issued through the Connecticut General Court. The instrument secured their possession of Milford’s lands, islands, woods, minerals, buildings, and other appurtenances according to their respective rights and shares. His inclusion establishes him as a recognized proprietor and landholder within the town.[^5]

Job lived through Milford’s transition from the New Haven Colony into the Connecticut Colony and through the gradual transformation of a precarious Puritan settlement into a mature colonial community. He was therefore more than the quiet patriarch of a large family. He served as a militia officer, taxpayer-assessor, auditor, land administrator, treasurer, and proprietor.

By the time of his death in 1715, the Gunns were no longer simply the children of English immigrants. Through Jobamah’s family, property, and public service, they had become firmly embedded in the life and governance of Milford.

**Through Jobamah Gunn, the family’s American story ceased to be primarily one of migration. It became a story of inheritance, public responsibility, and belonging.**
