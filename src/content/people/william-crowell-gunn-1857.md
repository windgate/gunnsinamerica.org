# William Crowell Gunn (1857–1922)

## A Builder of Towns

When William Crowell Gunn died at Fort Scott in April 1922, the loss was measured not only in the passing of a man, but in the recognition that a certain kind of life—expansive, energetic, and rooted in place—had reached its end. For more than forty years, he had been identified with the growth of Fort Scott and with enterprises extending across the Midwest and South. He dealt in land, railroads, natural gas, utilities, manufacturing, and finance. Yet the most enduring monument to his life was neither a company nor a fortune. It was the land he gave to the people of Fort Scott as Gunn Park.[^1]

William was not born into wealth. He was born at Keokuk, Iowa, on 29 May 1857, the son of the Reverend Elihu Gunn and Amy Barrett Gunn.[^2] His father’s ministry carried the family west through a world in which religious purpose did not necessarily provide material security. William’s childhood therefore followed a pattern already familiar in the Gunn family: strong expectations, limited resources, and an early demand for self-reliance.

He did not resist it. He answered it.

## Family and Education

William grew up in a large, closely connected family. Among his siblings were Amy Anna Gunn, who married Gilbert S. Warn; Abigail Florence “Abby” Gunn, who married William Baker and later became nationally known for her work with the Daughters of the American Revolution; Edward Gunn; and Charles Elihu Gunn. The surviving family portrait made at Fort Scott about 1890 places William and his wife, Loula, within the larger household of Elihu and Amy Gunn—a family shaped by ministry, education, work, and mutual obligation.[^3]

His education was broader than the hard circumstances of his youth might suggest. He attended the Kansas State Agricultural College at Manhattan, the State Normal School at Emporia, and the University of Kansas. The surviving accounts do not establish that he earned a degree from any of the three institutions. They do show, however, that he sought instruction in agriculture, education, and the broader world of public affairs before settling fully into business.[^4]

On 29 February 1882, according to the later newspaper biography, William married Loula Rice, the eldest daughter of General John H. Rice and his wife. That printed date presents a problem—1882 was not a leap year—and should be verified against the Bourbon County marriage record. The marriage itself and the year are consistently reported, even if the exact day remains uncertain.[^5]

William and Loula had two children:

- **William Rice Gunn**, who entered business with his father and later served as president of the Gunn-Buschow Burner Company. He married Irene Houston, daughter of John Lincoln Houston of Malta Bend, Missouri.
- **Nanna Loula Gunn**, who married William Kenneth Calhoun, one of the owners of the Calhoun Dry Goods Company of Fort Scott.[^6]

The family lived at 306 South Judson Street in Fort Scott. William’s home became known for its hospitality, just as his office became known as a place whose doors remained open to visitors. The public man and the private man were not separate figures. His instinct to gather people, listen to proposals, and offer help operated in both places.

## Beginning with the Railroad

William’s first employment was not in an office, bank, or investment house. It was along the railroad. As a young man, he worked as a section laborer on the Missouri–Kansas–Texas Railroad and later on a narrow-gauge line between Cherokee and Parsons, Kansas. The work was hard and unadorned, but it placed him within the larger force reshaping the country. Railroads did more than carry freight and passengers. They determined which towns would grow, which markets farmers could reach, and where capital and population would move.

For a time, William also worked on the large stock farm and dairy of his maternal uncle, J. R. Barrett, near Sedalia, Missouri. There he acquired a practical understanding of farming that later informed his judgment in land development and agricultural promotion.[^7]

At twenty-one, he returned to Fort Scott and entered the real-estate business. He helped establish the firm of Gunn & Marsh, which became the foundation of a career extending far beyond its local beginnings. After Marsh withdrew, William continued the business and eventually brought his son, William Rice Gunn, into the firm. From offices in Fort Scott and, at different times, Columbus, Parsons, El Dorado, and other Kansas communities, he conducted transactions across Kansas, Illinois, Nebraska, Missouri, Oklahoma, Texas, Alabama, and elsewhere.[^8]

William understood that the sale of land depended upon the movement of people. He advertised extensively in the more heavily populated states and encouraged families to settle in Kansas and other developing regions. His work belongs to the great redistribution of population and capital that transformed the American interior after the Civil War. He did not merely sell farms or town lots. He participated in the construction of communities.

## Enterprise as Reconstruction

William C. Gunn did not simply transact in land—he looked for ways to transform it.

In Pike County, Illinois, he acquired approximately six thousand acres described as swamp and underbrush. Through drainage and pumping, the tract was converted into productive farmland. Near the end of his life, he exchanged the remaining Illinois property for a six-thousand-acre wheat ranch in Montana, then divided and marketed that land to Iowa farmers. The transactions were commercial, but they also reveal the habit of mind that governed much of his work: where others saw exhausted, failed, or unusable property, he saw a system that might be reorganized and made productive.[^9]

That habit carried him into industry. One of Fort Scott’s unsuccessful ventures had been the Parkinson Sugar Works, created to manufacture granulated sugar from sorghum cane. After the experiment failed, William purchased the plant and reorganized it as the Fort Scott Sorghum Syrup Company. Under the revised plan, the factory became a substantial producer rather than an abandoned industrial shell.[^10]

He applied the same instinct to the Fort Scott Brick Company after it entered bankruptcy. Gunn purchased the stock, rebuilt the enterprise, and continued through fires, floods, and financial difficulty until the plant again stood on a sound footing. Only then did he sell it to new owners.[^11]

His interests also reached into transportation. In 1889 and 1890, he became a principal promoter, general manager, and major stockholder in the New Orleans, Natchez and Northwestern Railroad. The undertaking was characteristic of the age: ambitious, capital-intensive, and founded on the expectation that transportation would create development along its route.[^12]

Not every enterprise can be reconstructed fully from the surviving celebratory accounts, and their claims should be tested against company, court, and financial records. What is clear is that William’s reputation rested on his willingness to enter ventures at moments when failure appeared likely—and to see whether reorganization, investment, and decisive management could make them useful again.

## Bringing Natural Gas to Fort Scott

One of William’s largest regional projects began when natural gas was developed in southern Kansas. He recognized that inexpensive fuel could alter the future of Fort Scott and neighboring Nevada, Missouri. At considerable expense, he extended a pipeline from the producing fields of Kansas and Oklahoma to Fort Scott. The contemporary obituary credited the project with reducing the local price of gas from $1.25 to twenty-five cents, though those figures require confirmation in utility and municipal records.[^13]

He later extended service to Nevada and acquired the city’s utilities, including its electric plant, waterworks, and street railway. He improved and operated the properties before selling them to an eastern concern. His gas interests eventually extended into Texas, Oklahoma, and Alabama, and contemporary accounts described him as a recognized authority on the industry.[^14]

The importance of the undertaking was larger than fuel. Reliable, affordable power affected households, factories, transportation, and the ability of a city to attract new enterprise. William had moved from selling the ground on which communities stood to building the systems that allowed them to function.

## Stabilizing a Town

The clearest measure of William’s local importance came during moments of crisis. After the First National Bank of Fort Scott failed in 1909, business confidence in the city fell sharply. William purchased the bank’s assets from the receiver, an action the obituary credited with enabling depositors to be paid and the receivership to close.[^15]

The act was not disinterested—he acquired assets that might ultimately hold value—but neither was it merely private speculation. A bank failure could spread loss across households, merchants, farms, and businesses. By assuming risk when confidence had collapsed, he helped interrupt that chain of damage.

His business life repeatedly followed this pattern. A failed sugar works became a syrup factory. A bankrupt brick company returned to production. Wet Illinois land became farms. A threatened bank was liquidated without leaving depositors to absorb the full loss. These were not isolated adventures. They were responses to the instability of his time.

The late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries demanded people who could move between uncertainty and opportunity—who could see possibility in failure and stability in reconstruction. William was not alone in possessing that confidence, nor did every large undertaking of the period serve the public equally. His significance lies in the repeated direction of his effort: toward making land productive, restoring employment, extending infrastructure, and sustaining the town in which he lived.

## Invention and Industry

During the final decade of his life, William supported a fuel-saving device invented by W. H. Palmer, formerly of Fort Scott. Convinced of its potential, he provided advice, capital, and organization. The resulting Gunn-Buschow Burner Company was based in Kansas City, with William’s son, Rice Gunn, as president. By the time of William’s death, the company claimed installations in more than five hundred power plants and used much of the output of the Fort Scott Foundry.[^16]

The enterprise joined two sides of William’s character: his attraction to practical invention and his insistence that an undertaking should create a wider network of work. A device developed by one man became a manufacturing concern, supported a local foundry, employed labor, and entered power plants across the country.

## Faith and Civic Responsibility

The religious world of William’s childhood left a lasting mark. He became a member and active supporter of the First Presbyterian Church of Fort Scott, but his giving crossed denominational lines. Contemporary accounts credited him with contributing to the construction of churches throughout the city regardless of affiliation.[^17]

He helped organize the Fort Scott Young Men’s Christian Association and supported construction of its building. He contributed to the Salvation Army building and gave a fifteen-acre site for the Collegiate Institute south of town. He also quietly assisted young men who could not otherwise afford a college education. His obituary identified nine such students in the later years of his life, in addition to many others whom he helped begin businesses or survive personal difficulty.[^18]

He was a thirty-second-degree Mason, a Rotarian, a Modern Woodman, a member of the Ancient Order of United Workmen, and an Elk. He was a Republican, although his obituary said that business left him little time for active party politics.[^19] The list reflects the associational world of the Progressive Era: churches, lodges, service clubs, commercial bodies, and charities operating together as the informal architecture of civic life.

William’s generosity was not separate from his business philosophy. Both rested on a belief that private success carried a public obligation. His wealth did not remove him from the community. It enlarged the circle of his responsibility.

## Gunn Park

The most visible expression of that responsibility came on 3 June 1910, when William gave Fern Lake and the Stewart farm to the City of Fort Scott for use as a public park.[^20] Additional land created what the city remembered as one of the largest natural parks in Kansas. William also funded road improvements and a rest house. The park later grew to approximately 155 acres, with lakes, wooded trails, shelters, recreation grounds, and access to the Marmaton River.[^21]

Gunn Park was not simply a gift of unused acreage. It belonged to a growing national conviction that crowded industrial towns needed common ground—places for recreation, health, gathering, and contact with the natural world. William could have divided and sold the land. Instead, he removed it from private development and gave it to the city.

It was an act of continuity: an acknowledgment that what had been accumulated in one generation would be lived in by the next.

## Death and Memory

William’s health declined during the final months of his life. He spent the winter receiving treatment at the Battle Creek Sanitarium and underwent an operation at Mercy Hospital in February. When physicians concluded that he could not recover, he returned to his home at 306 South Judson Street. He died there at 6:15 in the evening on Wednesday, 12 April 1922, six weeks before his sixty-fifth birthday.[^22]

Funeral services were held at the First Presbyterian Church, followed by burial in Evergreen Cemetery. His wife, Loula; their children, William Rice Gunn and Nanna Loula Gunn Calhoun; his sisters Abby Gunn Baker and Amy Gunn Warn; and his brother Charles survived him.[^23]

The long obituary published after his death was openly admiring, as such notices often were. Yet its catalogue of enterprises and gifts is valuable because it reveals how Fort Scott understood him. He was remembered not simply as a wealthy man, but as a person whose energies had entered the physical life of the town—its fuel, factories, streets, banks, institutions, and park.

His life also carried forward an older inheritance. From the Gunn tradition came the habit of practical engagement: the willingness to work within the material conditions of life. From his father’s ministry came the expectation that such work carried moral weight. In William those elements appeared in the language of a different century—enterprise instead of subsistence, infrastructure instead of first settlement, public institutions instead of the early meetinghouse.

The form had changed. The pattern had not.

William began with little, worked without assurance, and met a period of expansion, instability, and technological change with engagement rather than retreat. His life was not significant because it stood apart from its time. It is significant because the larger movement of his generation can be seen within it: the transition from railroad labor to organized enterprise, from frontier development to civic structure, and from private accumulation to public inheritance.

By his lifetime, the River had widened. It no longer moved only through households and small settlements, but through towns, industries, and institutions. In that current, William Crowell Gunn took his place. The ground beneath the next generation was made more secure—and part of it was set aside, permanently, for everyone.

## Research Notes

The newspaper date requires careful reading. One transcription rendered the year as **1921**, but the internal chronology establishes **1922**: 14 April fell on a Friday in 1922, the article says Gunn died on Wednesday, and it reports that he was sixty-four and would have turned sixty-five on 29 May. Taken together, those facts place his death on **12 April 1922** and support the birth date **29 May 1857** found in the 1912 Kansas cyclopedia. Some derivative memorial pages give 1921 and should not be preferred over the internally consistent contemporary notice without further evidence.

The obituary gives the marriage date as “February 29, 1882.” Because 1882 was not a leap year, the exact day is impossible as printed. The Bourbon County civil marriage record or contemporaneous newspaper notice should be obtained before a precise date is published as fact.

The obituary is an unusually detailed but commemorative source. Its claims about investment totals, industrial output, numbers of settlers, burner installations, educational beneficiaries, and the disposition of failed-bank assets should be compared with deeds, corporate records, court files, city minutes, and newspapers before being treated as audited figures.
