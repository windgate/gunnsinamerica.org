# Elizabeth Sarah “Lizzie” Gunn Ayres

*Diarist, pioneer daughter, and Kansas civic leader*

<img src="/images/people/elizabeth-gunn-ayres.jpg" alt="Elizabeth Sarah “Lizzie” Gunn Ayres" style="float: left; width: 50%; height: auto; margin: 0.25rem 1.5rem 1rem 0;" />

Elizabeth Sarah “Lizzie” Gunn Ayres was born into one frontier community and helped build the institutions of another. Her father, Aaron Gunn, had traveled from Massachusetts to the Illinois country when much of La Salle was still unsettled by newcomers. Her husband, Franklin Langworthy Ayres, was the son of another early La Salle settler. Together they followed the westward movement into Kansas, where Lizzie became known for work that seldom made headlines but gave a growing community much of its character: organizing women, caring for neighbors, and serving steadily through church and civic life.

Her surviving diary offers a rare view of her earlier years. Long before newspapers recorded her work in Kansas, Lizzie set down her own observations during a journey east in 1867. Through it we meet not only a daughter, wife, and mother, but a young woman attentive to the people and places that had shaped her family’s past.[1]

## Aaron and Nancy: A Pioneer Household

Lizzie was a daughter of **Aaron Gunn (1806–1897)** and **Nancy Winters Gunn (1806–1905)**. Her birth is generally placed in 1846, although a compiled family account gives 2 February 1847; the original family record has yet to be found to settle the question.[2]

Aaron was born in Montague, Massachusetts, in the Gunn line descended from Jasper Gunn, who arrived in New England aboard the *Defence* in 1635. In 1831 he traveled west with members of a Massachusetts colony bound for Illinois. An account published in 1877 recalls Aaron and seven other young men making part of the journey by canoe, descending the St. Joseph River, crossing a portage, and continuing along the Kankakee and Illinois rivers. The Black Hawk War disrupted his early efforts to establish a farm. In 1835 he purchased land north of La Salle, and in 1837 he married Nancy Winters of Mount Palatine. Their household grew alongside the town.[3]

Nancy had been born in Ohio. Aaron’s story survives in accounts of migration, land, and settlement; much of Nancy’s work is less visible: raising children and sustaining a household through decades of change. Both parents lived long enough to see the frontier settlement they knew become an established Illinois town.

## Brothers and Sisters

The 1877 *History of La Salle County* identifies seven children of Aaron and Nancy Gunn: **Lydia C.**, who died at eighteen; **Jennett Z. (“Nettie”)**, wife of George A. Elliott; **Moses Winters**; **Lucy G.**, wife of Heman B. Chapman; **Elizabeth Sarah**; **Aaron Elihu**; and **Bella E. (“Belle”)**, then living at home.[3] Later family compilations supply additional dates and name Belle’s husband as Thomas A. Williams, though these details still warrant comparison with original records.[2]

Two of Lizzie’s brothers illustrate the different directions taken by the children of the Illinois pioneers.

**Moses Winters Gunn (1839–1935)**, born in La Salle on 31 March 1839, remained closely tied to the region. He attended local schools and studied for a year at Franklin College in Indiana. During the Civil War he served in Company K of the Eleventh Illinois Infantry, enlisting in 1864 and mustering out in July 1865. His service included the campaign around Mobile and the battle of Fort Blakely. The 1877 county history identifies him as pastor of the Baptist church at Normal, Illinois, and a later biographical account also emphasizes his farming and his long religious work. He married Emma Jane Richards. In his extraordinary longevity, Moses connected the La Salle of the early settlers with the twentieth century.[3][4]

**Aaron Elihu Gunn (1848–1890)**, born on 14 March 1848, followed the movement outward. He attended Hillsdale College, worked in telegraphy, and married Mary Ann Williams. In the spring of 1886 they moved with their children to Chappell, Nebraska, where he died in March 1890, just short of his forty-second birthday. His obituary in the *Chappell Register* recalled a man active in Sunday school and church work and remembered by those who knew him for his gentle temper. It named as survivors his wife and five children, his aged parents and a brother in La Salle. Among the children was **[Nina Belle Gunn](/people/nina-belle-gunn-pyle/) (1877–1941)**, through whom this branch of the family continues in the direct line traced in *The Gunns in America*.[5]

Moses stayed close to the country his parents had settled; Aaron Elihu moved farther west with the railroads and communications systems. Lizzie’s course would take her to Kansas, where her work took another form: building a community’s social and charitable institutions.

## The Diary of 1867

In 1867, before her marriage, Lizzie traveled east through a network of relatives and places connected with her family’s history, and her diary records the journey in her own voice. One of its most meaningful destinations was Montague, Massachusetts, where her father had been born and where earlier generations of Gunns had lived and were buried.[1]

She recorded a visit to the old burial ground, “where so many of our family lie.” The passage is brief, but its significance is considerable. For a young woman raised in Illinois, Montague was not merely a place on a map. It was the landscape her father had left and where generations before him had made their homes.

The diary also deepens our understanding of Lizzie herself. Genealogical records tell us whom she married and where she lived. Her journal lets us see what caught her attention before marriage and motherhood came to dominate the surviving record.

## Marriage: Two La Salle Families

On **5 January 1868**, Lizzie married **Franklin Langworthy Ayres (1844–1926)** at the First Baptist Church in La Salle.[6] Franklin was the son of **Burton Ayres (1806–1870)** and **Orilla Langworthy**, whose given name appears as *Aurelia* in some family material. Burton had come from Ohio to La Salle in 1830 and worked as a blacksmith and farmer. The 1877 county history places his shop near the Little Vermilion River and counts his family among the locality’s first settlers.[3]

Aaron Gunn and Burton Ayres belonged to the generation that made the beginnings. Their children inherited a town with farms, businesses, churches, roads, and growing connections to the country beyond it. Franklin ran a small drug business in La Salle before turning his attention westward. In a newspaper interview many years later, he remembered returning to find that familiar names had given way to those of their sons: another generation had taken up the work.[7]

Their marriage joined two families whose experience of settlement had much in common. But it was not to keep Lizzie and Franklin in Illinois.

## The Move to Kansas

Franklin went west in the autumn of 1871 and returned the following year to bring his family. By the middle of the decade the Ayres household was established near Indianola in Butler County, Kansas.[7] Land could be claimed and cultivated, but a community required more than farms. It needed schools, churches, places to gather, and ways to help neighbors in difficulty.

Lizzie devoted herself to that work. Local accounts credit her with organizing an early women’s club in Butler County in **1878**. Women’s associations offered education, companionship, and collective action at a time when women’s formal political participation was restricted, and a meeting begun in a home could eventually shape the life of an entire town.[8]

Lizzie and Franklin raised a large family amid these changes. Family tradition speaks of **eight children**, and the family record bears it out. The anniversary record prepared for their fiftieth wedding anniversary in 1918 names seven living or remembered children.[6] The eighth, their firstborn, **Allan Brainard Ayres**, died young in 1877, which accounts for the difference.[11]

The children of Franklin and Lizzie were:

- **Allan Brainard Ayres** (1868–1877), who died as a child.
- **Charlotte “Lottie” Belle Ayres** (1870–1950), who married Merritt A. Nelson (1855–1947).
- **Stella Elizabeth Ayres** (died 1904), who married Anderson Holladay (1848–1940).
- **Warren Elliott Ayres** (1873–1947), who married Susie Granny Fearhake (1872–1957).
- **Melton Durley Ayres** (1877–1950), who married Edith B. Smith (1879–1952).
- **Jessie Evelyn Ayres** (1881–1976), who married Franklin “Frank” Corbin (1873–1941).
- **Franklin Burton Ayres** (1884–1957), who married Winona Edith Johns (1904–1993).
- **Stanley Edwin Ayres** (1891–1969), who married Maud Salome Long (1887–1946).[11]

The children carried forward the Ayres, Gunn, Winters, and Langworthy families, and their names preserve traces of kinship. Franklin Burton recalled father and grandfather; Warren Elliott and Melton Durley suggest wider family connections, though the reasons for those names have not been documented.

## Women’s Work Becomes Public Work

In Kansas, Lizzie’s efforts extended well beyond her household. She became closely associated with the **Woman’s Christian Temperance Union (WCTU)**, whose local work encompassed temperance, religious instruction, charitable relief, and civic improvement.

A surviving newspaper account dates the organization of the Augusta WCTU to **10 August 1886**, when ten women gathered in Lizzie’s home. She served the organization for many years, including a term as local president. The work was done through meetings, correspondence, fundraising, and personal visits, often without the recognition given to elected office.[9]

The union helped provide a room in the city building where women visiting Augusta could rest and gather. Its members assisted people in need and supported improvements to community life, including efforts associated with a public library. These were practical accomplishments: in a growing town, a dedicated room, a place to seek help, and an organization prepared to respond could make a real difference.

Around 1900 the family settled in Augusta, where Lizzie’s service became especially visible. She taught Sunday School, worked with the Friendly Philathea class, and took part in the church’s Home Department, which kept in touch with people unable to attend services. Ministers and missionaries were welcomed into the Ayres home, as were people seeking assistance.[8]

Her faith and her public service were inseparable. The same sense of obligation that sustained her household found expression in the organizations she helped lead.

## A Niece in the Household

Lizzie’s home also sheltered the next generation of her own family. **[Nina Belle Gunn](/people/nina-belle-gunn-pyle/) (1877–1941)** was the daughter of Lizzie’s late brother Aaron Elihu Gunn, who had drowned in Nebraska in 1890. From 1899 to 1900, as she began her teaching career in Augusta, Nina lived with Lizzie and Franklin.[5][10]

On Sunday afternoon, 10 June 1900, at three o’clock, Nina married William H. Pyle of Towanda, Kansas, at the Ayres home. A local newspaper called it “a very pretty wedding” and described the bride as a niece of Mrs. Ayres. The house that had welcomed ministers, missionaries, and neighbors in need now opened for a family celebration.[10]

Nina is the link through which this branch of the Gunn family continues in the direct line traced in *The Gunns in America*. Her years in Augusta tie that line back to Lizzie, the aunt who gave her a home at the start of her working life and a place to be married.

## A Long Marriage and a Lasting Influence

On 5 January 1918, Lizzie and Franklin marked **fifty years of marriage**. The anniversary record preserves the names of their children and the places the family had lived, from La Salle to Indianola and Augusta.[6] The daughter of an Illinois pioneer had spent much of her adult life in Kansas, helping give a growing town the social structures of a settled community.

**Elizabeth Sarah Gunn Ayres died on 2 March 1923** in Augusta after a brief illness. Her obituary in the *Augusta Daily Gazette* on 6 March described her many years of church and community work. Her funeral was held at the First Baptist Church. Franklin survived her until 1926.[8]

No single event explains the importance of Lizzie’s life. Its meaning lies in accumulation: the home opened to others, the women brought together, the Sunday School class taught, the organization sustained, the neighbor helped. Her father had helped establish a settlement in Illinois. Lizzie helped turn a settlement in Kansas into a functioning community.

Her brothers carried their inheritance into military service, ministry, farming, telegraphy, and westward migration. Lizzie carried hers into forms of public work too easily overlooked because so much of it began in the home. Her diary lets us see the young woman before that work began; the later record shows what she made of the years that followed.

In Elizabeth Sarah Gunn Ayres, the work of one generation did not end. It extended outward.
