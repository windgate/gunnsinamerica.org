Anna Sartwell Gunn was born on 21 January 1760 in Sunderland, Massachusetts, the daughter of Nathaniel Sartwell and Hannah Gunn. Known also as Ann, she was among the early settlers of Ohio's Western Reserve. Her life connected the Gunn family's New England beginnings with its westward migration, and her care for neighbors helped sustain the small settlement that became Cleveland.

## Family and Marriage

Anna's mother, Hannah Gunn, was the daughter of Deacon John Gunn and Hannah Root. John descended from Jasper Gunn through Jasper's son Nathaniel and grandson Deacon Samuel Gunn.

About 1780, Anna married [Elijah Gunn](/people/elijah-gunn-sr-1759/) (1759–1855), her second cousin. Both were great-grandchildren of Deacon Samuel Gunn and Elizabeth Wyatt. Anna's line passed through their son Deacon John Gunn and his daughter Hannah; Elijah's passed through their son Deacon Nathaniel Gunn and his son Lieutenant Nathaniel Gunn.

Anna was fifteen when the American Revolution began. Her father, Nathaniel Sartwell, responded to the Lexington alarm in April 1775 and subsequently served as a lieutenant in the Massachusetts forces. The surviving accounts do not describe Anna's experiences during the war, but her early adulthood and marriage fell within those years of upheaval.

Anna and Elijah had six children:

- Philena Gunn (1780–1845)
- Charles Gunn (1780–1832)
- Christopher Gunn (1783–1850)
- Horace Gunn (1786–1876)
- Elijah Gunn Jr. (1795–1875)
- Minerva Gunn (1800–1824)

Five were born before the family's journey to Ohio. Minerva, their youngest, was born after Anna and Elijah had established themselves on the Western Reserve.

## The Journey to Ohio

In 1796, Anna and Elijah traveled west with the Connecticut Land Company's surveying expedition. Anna was thirty-six years old.

Amzi Atwater, a member of the expedition, later recalled that the party reached Conneaut on 4 July 1796. His account identifies Elijah Gunn, his wife, and a young son among the travelers. It does not establish when the couple's other children joined them in Ohio.

Anna and Elijah remained at Conneaut when the surveying parties dispersed to their work. They took charge of the company's supply station, known as Stow's Castle, and stayed after most of the surveyors returned east that autumn. Atwater remembered severe shortages of provisions and other necessities among the families left there during the winter of 1796–1797.

Anna and Elijah moved from Conneaut to Cleveland in the spring of 1797. They initially occupied a company cabin, followed by a cabin on River Street. They subsequently moved to the higher ground southeast of the settlement, in the area that became Newburgh.

Anna appears by name in the Connecticut Land Company's proceedings. At its meeting in Hartford on 23 January 1798, the company approved a grant of one hundred acres to "Anna Gun, wife of Elijah Gun." The entry provides a direct record of her place among the settlement's early land recipients.

## Caring for the Settlement

Illness shaped daily life in early Cleveland. During the fever season of 1798, entire households were sick, and obtaining food, water, and firewood became difficult. An account preserved in Charles Whittlesey's *Early History of Cleveland*, drawing on Judge John Barr's manuscripts, describes the Gunn and Kingsbury families on the ridge as comparatively healthy and repeatedly visiting the settlement to help their sick neighbors.

This account, published in 1867, provides evidence of the Gunn family's caregiving decades before Gertrude Van Rensselaer Wickham wrote about Anna. Wickham later remembered her specifically as a skilled nurse and midwife, valued by neighbors and friends. Alongside the demands of her own household, Anna helped others through sickness and childbirth.

Wickham's recollections of Anna and her arrival in Cleveland are presented separately in the Voices section of this website.

## Later Years

The surviving accounts reveal less about Anna's later life. Whittlesey records that Elijah eventually left the Newburgh homestead to live with a son near Napoleon, on the Maumee River. His account does not establish whether Anna accompanied him.

Anna's death date and burial place remain unconfirmed. Elijah died on 22 September 1855 and is buried in Gunn Cemetery in Henry County, Ohio.

Anna's history survives in expedition recollections, company proceedings, family records, and accounts of neighbors helping one another. Together, they document a woman who shared responsibility for an early supply station, established a household in a new settlement, received a land grant in her own name, and helped care for a community struggling through illness.
