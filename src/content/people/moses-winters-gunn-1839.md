Moses Winters Gunn was born in a log cabin in La Salle, Illinois, on 31 March 1839. He died ninety-six years later, in October 1935, as the last surviving Civil War veteran in La Salle and the last member of Carter Post of the Grand Army of the Republic. Between those dates he was a farmer, a soldier in the final campaign of the war, a Baptist preacher, a husband and father, and for his last decades the community's living link to the Union Army.[^1]

## Early Life

Moses was one of seven children of Aaron Gunn (1806–1897) and Nancy Winters Gunn (1806–1905), New England descendants who settled in northern Illinois in the 1830s. He was born at what later became Eleventh and Chartres Streets in La Salle. According to the obituary, his father came west in 1833 with the Hampshire Colony, settled first near La Moille and then in Putnam County, and established the family homestead in La Salle in 1838.[^2]

Moses grew up on the farm and helped his father work it. The obituary reports that he spent a year at Hillsdale College in Michigan before the war interrupted his schooling.[^3]

Two of his siblings appear elsewhere on this site. His sister [Elizabeth Sarah "Lizzie" Gunn Ayres](/people/elizabeth-sarah-gunn-ayres-1846/) (1846–1923) moved to Kansas and became a diarist and civic organizer there. His brother Aaron Elihu Gunn (1848–1890) went to Nebraska; Aaron Elihu's daughter was [Nina Belle Gunn Pyle](/people/nina-belle-gunn-pyle/) (1877–1941), through whom the direct line traced in *The Gunns in America* continues.[^4]

## A Soldier in the Union Army

Moses left La Salle on 1 October 1864, at twenty-five, to enter the Union Army. The regimental roster places him in Company K of the 11th Illinois Infantry, which was raised in 1861 and drew most of its companies from La Salle County. He joined it as a recruit, and was mustered out with it on 14 July 1865.[^5]

The obituary places him in the operations against Mobile, Alabama, including Spanish Fort and Fort Blakely. The regimental record supports this. The 11th Illinois, under Colonel James H. Coates, belonged to the Second Brigade, First Division, Thirteenth Army Corps, and took part in the siege of both works. Spanish Fort fell on 8 April 1865. Fort Blakely was stormed the next day, and Union troops occupied Mobile on 12 April.[^6]

The regiment was mustered out at Baton Rouge, Louisiana, on 14 July 1865, and went on to Springfield for payment and final discharge. Moses's obituary gives 14 July as the day he returned to La Salle, which appears to merge the muster-out with the homecoming; the date of his arrival home is not recorded.[^7]

The obituary says nothing of his own experience of the campaign. His pension file and the regiment's own records may fill that gap. His commander is named in the obituary only as "General Conly," a name that has not been matched to any officer.[^6]

## Farmer and Preacher

After the war Moses helped his father manage the family's affairs and farmed near Troy Grove and north of La Salle. He also studied theology on his own, and the study led him into the Baptist ministry.[^8]

In June 1874 he became pastor of a Baptist church in Troy Grove, and later served a congregation in Norman Township, Grundy County. Poor health ended his regular pastoral work after a short time, and for several years he could not keep up an active ministry. He remained connected to Baptist congregations. He helped organize the Oglesby Union Church and preached the sermon at its dedication, and he served as supply pastor at a church in Marseilles, though family responsibilities kept him from taking a permanent call there. For many years he also helped distribute religious tracts and copies of the New Testament.[^8] A 1975 newspaper feature on early leaders of the First Baptist Church of La Salle pictured him with its pastor of 1902–1910, the Rev. William H. Beeby, and credited the Gunn family with contributing much to the church in time and funds in its early years.[^15]

Church leadership ran deep in his ancestry. Deacons appear in four successive generations of his direct line: Samuel Gunn, [Nathaniel Gunn Sr.](/people/nathaniel-gunn-1693/) (1693–1779), [Nathaniel Gunn Jr.](/people/nathaniel-gunn-1726/) (1726–1807), and Elihu Gunn (1763–1851).[^9]

## Marriage and Family

On 14 May 1889, at fifty, Moses married Emma Jane Richards, daughter of David Richards and his wife, pioneer residents of Fairfield Township in Bureau County, Illinois.[^10] The couple had four children:

| [Carroll David Gunn](/voices/carroll-gunn-oral-history-1976/) | 1896–1980 | Living in La Salle in 1935. Narrator of a 1976 audio oral history. |
| Emory Lloyd Gunn | 1898–1963 | Called Lloyd in the obituary; living in Omaha, Nebraska, in 1935. |

The surviving children's residences are those given in the 1935 obituary.[^11]

In 1904 the family moved to 134 Gunn Avenue in La Salle, which remained Moses's home for the rest of his active life.[^12]

## The Last Veteran of La Salle

As the decades passed, the Grand Army of the Republic shrank to a handful of old men. Moses belonged to Carter Post in La Salle, and at Memorial Day observances he was regularly recognized for his service. He eventually became the last Civil War veteran in La Salle and the last surviving member of Carter Post. As its final member, he kept the post's flag.[^12]

### Sidebar: Carter Post and the Grand Army of the Republic

The Grand Army of the Republic was founded in 1866 at Decatur, Illinois, as a fraternal organization for Union veterans. Its local posts served as centers of fellowship, remembrance, and public commemoration.

Moses belonged to Carter Post in La Salle. By 1935 he was its last member and held its flag. The flag was displayed at his funeral, where an honorary escort from the American Legion represented a younger generation of veterans.

## Final Years

In 1927 Moses fell at home and fractured his hip. The injury required several months in the hospital and ended his physical independence, but he lived another eight years. By the summer of 1935 he was a resident of the Mrs. Thomas Rendle Home for Aged Persons in Ottawa, Illinois, and his health failed during the last week of his life.[^12]

Moses Winters Gunn died on Friday, 18 October 1935, aged ninety-six. Longevity ran in the family: his father, Aaron, died on 12 March 1897 at ninety, and his mother, Nancy, lived to ninety-nine.[^13]

He was survived by his wife, Emma, and three of his four children: Frances Ivy Gunn of Chicago, Carroll David Gunn of La Salle, and Emory Lloyd Gunn of Omaha. Wilmer had died more than forty years earlier. Members of the Romulus Meehan Post of the American Legion served as honorary escort at the funeral, and the flag of Carter Post was displayed during the services.[^12]

### Sidebar: The Gilded Generation

In the generational framework of William Strauss and Neil Howe, Moses belongs to the Gilded Generation (born 1822–1842), a "Nomad" cohort that came of age during the sectional crisis and the Civil War. His life spanned the settlement of the Illinois frontier, the war, the postwar religious and civic life of the Midwest, and the era of automobiles and radio. The framework supplies historical context only; it is interpretive and is not genealogical evidence.[^14]
