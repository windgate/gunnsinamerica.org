Franklin Burton Ayres (1884–1957), known as Burton, was the son of Franklin Langworthy Ayres and Elizabeth "Lizzie" Gunn Ayres, and a grandson of the La Salle County pioneer Burton Ayres. He grew up on his parents' farm in Butler County, Kansas, and served in France in 1918 and 1919 as a sergeant with an army ambulance company.

## A Kansas Boyhood

Franklin and Lizzie brought their family from La Salle County, Illinois, to a farm in Butler County, Kansas. A family newspaper account preserves a few glimpses of Burton's older siblings. The eldest, Allan, was three when they arrived. His mother found him beside a stone, pointing at a coiled rattlesnake and calling it "pretty." She got to him in time, but Allan died in an accident about five years later. The eldest daughter, Charlotte, was given to romancing. She once told the hired girl a tale of a big girl and a little girl fleeing a stampede of escaped ponies, and then admitted the little girl was her sister Stella and the big girl was herself.

## Service in France, 1918–1919

Burton was mustered in at Fort Logan, Colorado, and sent to Fort Riley, Kansas. There he spent about eleven months training with Ambulance Company 18, then joined a newly organized unit, Ambulance Company 41, attached to the regular army. The company left Fort Riley on 3 July 1918 and entered France at Brest.

Fort Riley had its own place in the war's history that spring. In early March 1918 an outbreak of influenza at Camp Funston, on the post, sickened hundreds of soldiers within days. It is among the earliest well-documented outbreaks of the pandemic that went on to kill millions worldwide. Burton's account does not say whether he was at Fort Riley when it struck.

The company arrived in France in time for the Ourcq-Vesle offensive, the second phase of the Battle of the Marne, and was then shifted to the St. Mihiel sector. After the salient was reduced, the company travelled through the darkest nights Burton ever expected to see to the Meuse-Argonne. It was the first fighting that seemed like war to them. They came under machine-gun fire, bombing from German planes and artillery bombardment, went days without sleep, and found the dead and wounded lying over meadows and in the woods. The fighting lasted from 26 September until the Armistice on 11 November 1918, apart from two weeks in a rest camp ten miles back.

On Armistice Day the company was in an abandoned German camp at Apremont, where the Germans had dug labyrinths of tunnels into the bluff for defense. From there the company worked back by stages to St. Nazaire, the port of embarkation. Burton later called his eight months in France a time of greater change than any he had known before or since. As a sergeant in a casual detachment, he was mustered out on 30 March 1919.

The men were not allowed to write home about what they saw, and most were reticent afterward. Burton was reported missing at one point, and his family spent some troubled weeks. The account notes that those waiting at home imagined terrible things, and doubts that the imagining was terrible enough.

## The Washboard

Among the things Burton brought home was a washboard he had made for himself, cutting the grooves into a pine board with his pocket knife. He described the French village laundry as primitive. The women came to the stream, rubbed their clothes on the rocks without soap, and spent the day washing, laughing and chatting. Burton washed his in the same stream, but with soap and probably more muscle. His mother said his clothes were whiter than anything in the house when he got home.
